Payment recovery

When a member's payment fails, the gym owner finds out the same day on the screen he already opens — and puts it right from there, by retrying the card or asking the member for a new one.

A worked example: an illustrative shaping document derived from published source material, not a real project. Source: Ryan Singer, End-to-end with Shape Up: a real-world case study (2025) (skill/references/03-articles/end-to-end-with-shape-up-a-real-world-case-study.md).

Why this matters

The framing

The problem this project sets out to solve, and how we will know it is solved — told in the words of the people who live it.

The struggling moment

The person who understands this best does not run a gym any more — he sells our product, and before that he ran one himself: a single room, one of every machine, a couple of hundred members whose names he knew. We asked him what he had to stay on top of back then. He did not describe a screen. He opened a spreadsheet he had kept for years.

One column in that sheet he touched every week: members whose payment had not gone through. He kept it because nothing else would tell him. A card expires on the 3rd. The charge fails that night. Nothing happens — no message, no mark on the screen he opens every morning. The member comes in on the 4th, on the 6th, on the 11th, says hello, trains, and goes home, and he says hello back, because he has no way of knowing. He finds out when he next sits down to rebuild the sheet: reports, then the sales report, then switch on a filter. By then it is three weeks of training nobody paid for, and the conversation he has to have is not your card bounced last night. It is you owe me for the month.

Today's way, and where it breaks

The first screen after signing in is the Overview. It carries three figures for the last fortnight — new members, bookings made, revenue — and three panels underneath them: new users, latest sales, and what is on today. For a gym with one owner, that is a list of things he already knows. He met every new member at the door. He rang up every sale himself.

The one thing he does not know is the money that did not arrive, and that is the one thing the Overview does not carry. It is in the system — a membership payment that failed is recorded, with the date and the reason — but reaching it is a route he has to remember and repeat.

So it breaks twice, and the second break is the worse one. Even when he finds the failed payment, the software stops there. There is no way to charge the card again — which is all that is needed when the payment failed for want of funds on a Tuesday and the member is paid on Friday — and no way to ask the member for a new card. The screen tells him to contact the customer and leaves him to it.

What people do instead is what he did: keep the sheet, and chase the money by hand. A text message. A name written on a card taped under the desk. A word with the member while he is stretching, which is the conversation nobody wants to have and everybody puts off — and the longer it is put off, the bigger the number in it.

The outcome test

A month after this is in front of owners, three checks anyone can run without asking us:

  1. Ask an owner when the last failed membership payment happened. He answers from memory or from the Overview — not by going and running a report — because the software told him the day it happened.

  2. Pick any failed membership payment from the last month and ask what was done about it. There is an answer written on the invoice: charged again on this day, a new card asked for on that day, paid on this one. Nobody had to remember to write it down.

  3. Ask him for the spreadsheet. He can find it, and cannot think of a reason to open it.

Behind those three, the number that pays for the project: of the membership payments that fail in a month, fewer are still unpaid thirty days later. We are not putting a target on it here — the first month with the panel is the baseline, and there is no baseline today because nobody is counting.

Why now

This pays in money, twice over. Every membership payment that fails and is never chased is recurring revenue that walks out of the door still holding a key fob, and today the only thing standing between that and the business is whether an owner happened to run a report. That is the first half.

The second half is that the leads are arriving now. Interest in this product is up, and we think more of it should be converting than does. Handling money that does not arrive is one of the few things this product is sold on doing well, and it is not handled well today — so much so that the Overview, the first screen anyone sees, is the screen our own salespeople skip past in a demo. Fixing the money on that screen fixes the demo on the same day.

The big hire and the little hires

The big hire is the owner, and it happens once, in about a week. He will run the panel and the spreadsheet side by side — he would be foolish not to — and at the end of that week he either closes the sheet for good or he never closes it at all. What has to be true is completeness, not cleverness: every member the sheet has, the panel has, on the same day, with nothing stale sitting in it. One row missing in that first week and the sheet comes back and stays back.

The little hires are every ordinary day afterwards, and they belong to more people than the owner.

The morning look at the Overview. It has to answer without a click: who, what was due, and what we last did about it. If he has to open a row to find out whether he has already dealt with it, he stops looking at the panel within a fortnight.

The alert that arrives when a payment fails. If it fires for payments that the automatic attempts then collect on their own, he learns to ignore it, and the one that mattered goes with the rest.

The member standing at the desk. Whoever is on the desk — the owner, his weekend cover, the coach who opens on Saturday — has to be able to take a new card there and then, in the time it takes to say goodbye. If the only route is to send an email to somebody standing in front of you, the card gets written on paper instead, and nobody types it in.

What we will build

The shape

The solution at the level of what people see and do. The drawings are rough sketches of how it works, not of how it will look.

In plain language

The project turned once while we were shaping it, and the turn is the shape. Surfacing the failed payments — putting the list where he can see it — is half a day's relief and then he is back to the phone. The moment we looked at what he would do next, the project moved.

So there are two halves here, knowing and doing, and neither is worth much without the other.

Knowing. Where the latest-sales panel is now, the Overview gets a panel headed Failed payments. One row per unpaid membership invoice: the member's name, the date it was due, and — the line that makes the panel worth reading rather than worth dreading — what we last did about it and when. A new message goes to the owner the day a membership payment fails, so the panel is not something he has to remember to look at.

Doing. A row leads to the membership's bill for that month — the contract invoice — on a page of its own, and that page carries two buttons. Retry now charges the card again while he watches, and tells him it worked, it failed, or it was refused outright. Ask for a card sends the member a short message that lands on one short screen where they type a card in and submit it. The card goes on the membership, not on the one bill, so next month's payment uses it too.

The same Ask for a card also sits on the member's membership tab, where there is no failed bill to hang it on — because that is the version the desk needs when the member is standing right there.

Finding it

The Overview loses one panel and gains one. Nothing else on that screen moves: the three figures across the top stay, new users stays, what is on today stays. The panel only ever lists membership payments — a card declined at the desk for a tub of protein is not a debt, and putting it here would teach him to skim the panel.

Rows are ordered by the date the invoice was due, oldest first, so the one that has been rotting longest is the one at the top. Each row is a link, whole and entire — there is no small chevron to hunt for.

▸ Data Where a failed membership payment actually lives

It is spread across four things that already exist, and no new table is needed to list it. A member holds a contract; the contract raises an invoice on a schedule; each attempt to collect an invoice is a sale. A failed membership payment is an unpaid invoice on a live contract whose most recent sale did not go through. 

member -> contract -> invoice -> sale (attempt)

panel row = invoice
  where invoice.paid_at is null
    and contract.state = 'active'
    and sale.kind = 'contract'
  order by invoice.due_on asc

The sale.kind filter is what keeps retail and one-off invoice sales out of the panel. It is the same filter the buried report already applies once someone finds the switch.

▸ Data The last-action line

No new column on the invoice. Every attempt, message and request already writes a history item against the invoice; the row's last line is the newest history item marked as worth alerting on, rendered as its summary plus a relative date. 

history_item(
  invoice_id   the invoice it happened to
  kind         attempt | card_requested | message_sent | paid
  summary      one line, written when the item is created
  alerting     true if it belongs on the panel row
  occurred_at  timestamp
)

Writing the summary at the moment the item is created, rather than composing it when the panel renders, is deliberate: the panel must be able to say what happened even for kinds of history nobody has thought of yet.

The alert, and charging the card again

When a membership payment fails, the owner gets a message naming the member and the invoice, with a link straight to it. It goes out once per invoice, on the first failure. The automatic attempts that follow show up on the row as last actions, not as more messages — the alert exists to make him look, and an alert he has learned to ignore has stopped doing that.

On the invoice, Retry now puts the charge through while he waits. It is one of three answers: it worked, it failed again, or the card was refused outright and no amount of trying will fix it. Whichever it is, the attempt is written into the invoice's history and becomes the row's new last action, so the panel tells him tomorrow what he did today.

▸ Contract What a manual retry is allowed to touch

A retry from this screen is an extra attempt sitting beside the automatic schedule, never a replacement for it. Two things it must never do, because both would quietly break collection for every gym on the product. 

retryContractInvoice(invoice_id)
  -> 'paid' | 'failed' | 'refused'

always:  writes one history_item, alerting = true
never:   marks the sale failed
never:   advances, resets or cancels the automatic
         attempt schedule for the invoice

The sale is only marked failed by the schedule, on its final attempt. Until then the schedule keeps running underneath whatever the owner does by hand, and a hand retry that succeeds simply settles the invoice and the schedule finds nothing left to do.

Asking for a card

Sometimes trying again is pointless: the card is cancelled, or expired, or belongs to an account that closed. Ask for a card sends the member a short message that says what it is — we need a payment method for your membership — and does not read as a bill for one month, because it is not one. It lands on a single screen: card fields and a submit button, nothing else on it.

The same request starts from the member's membership tab, with no failed invoice involved. That is the version the desk uses, and it is why the screen has to be short: it will be handed across a counter on somebody's phone while a class is filing in.

▸ Contract The card request, and what the card lands on

The link the member follows is single-use and expiring, and it is tied to the contract rather than to a sale. That is the whole point: a card given to settle one bill and forgotten by next month solves nothing. 

payment_method_request(
  token        single use, expires after 14 days
  contract_id  the membership, never a sale
  requested_by staff member, or the system
  used_at      null until submitted
)

on submit:
  the card becomes the contract's payment method
  any unpaid invoice on that contract is charged
  a history_item is written on each of them

The existing failed-payment message to the member already reaches a hosted checkout page for the one sale. That flow stays exactly as it is; this is a second, plainer door beside it, and only the new one moves the payment method on the contract.

Walkthrough

The same man, the same gym, the same failed card, with this in place.

  1. Tuesday morning, before he unlocks the door, there is a message: a membership payment failed last night. It names the member and what was owed. He was going to open the app anyway.

  2. He opens the Overview. Where latest sales used to be there is Failed payments, and three rows in it. The one he was messaged about is at the top: the member's name, the date it was due, and underneath, automatic attempt failed — last night.

  3. He clicks the row. The invoice: what is owed, when it was due, and every attempt so far in order. He can see this is the second month in a row.

  4. He knows this member. The card was replaced last month after it was cloned, so charging the old one again is a waste of everybody's morning. He clicks Ask for a card. The member gets a message that says we need a payment method for your membership, not a demand for August.

  5. He goes back to the Overview. The row now reads card requested — today. There is nothing to write down and nothing to remember: if nothing comes back, the row is still there tomorrow saying exactly that.

  6. The member turns up at six for the class, having read the message on the bus and done nothing about it. The coach on the desk opens his membership tab, clicks Ask for a card, and turns the phone round. Card, submit, and he goes and warms up.

  7. The invoice is settled on the new card the moment it is submitted, and the card is the membership's from now on. Next morning the row is gone from the panel. The conversation about owing for the month never happens, because nobody ever got three weeks behind.

Time bombs

The places where this could blow up mid-build. Each one is defused here, in this document, before the build starts.

The page that shows a payment's detail today is shared by three different kinds of sale, and it is old.

How we defuse it We do not touch it. Contract invoices get a page of their own, carrying only what an owner needs in order to see and settle an unpaid membership payment, with one link out to the existing sale page for refunds and everything else. The old page keeps serving retail sales and one-off invoices exactly as it does today, and nothing we build depends on changing what its header shows.

▸ Spike What it would have taken to change the shared header

The question we took into the spike was whether the header on that page is a shared component. It is: one component renders for all three sale kinds, and what it shows is decided from the sale's kind at render time, with the retail and one-off paths reading fields the membership path does not set.

Adding a membership branch to it means touching the two paths we have no business touching, and their tests, in order to prove we did not. A new page for membership invoices costs less than the branch does, and it costs it once.

A retry the owner fires by hand colliding with the automatic attempts already running underneath.
How we defuse it A hand retry is an extra attempt, not a replacement: it never marks the sale failed, and it never advances, resets or cancels the schedule. Up to the final automatic attempt, the schedule keeps running whatever the owner does. And the alert to the owner goes out once per invoice, on the first failure — the attempts after it land on the row as last actions instead of as more messages, which is what stops the alert becoming noise he filters.
One member, several months unpaid.
How we defuse it The panel lists invoices, oldest due date first, so a member three months behind appears three times — which is the truth, and it is what a run of failures looks like. The invoice page shows the whole membership's invoices in order, so the run reads as a run rather than as three unrelated rows. Because the last-action line belongs to each invoice, a member who has already been asked for a card is not asked again by whoever opens the next row down.
A new card that pays the bill in front of us and nothing after it.
How we defuse it The request is tied to the membership, not to the sale that failed, and the message to the member says so in as many words. The card that comes back becomes the membership's payment method, settles whatever is outstanding on it, and is what next month's payment uses. A card that only cleared one invoice would put the same member back on the panel in thirty days and teach the owner that the button does not work.
The card is handed over at the desk, not typed into a message at home.
How we defuse it Ask for a card also lives on the member's membership tab and opens the same short screen, so whoever is on the desk can take the card on the spot. Without this the daily use falls apart: nobody sends an email to a person standing in front of them, so the card gets written on paper and typed in later, or never.

No-gos

Things this project deliberately does not do, so nobody has to wonder.

  • Class utilisation. It was in the frame and it came out of the frame. The subject-matter expert settled it himself when we said we were not sure both would fit: “the class utilization thing would be very useful, but I only told you about it because you said you needed more” (the case study). Solving missed payments is the whole project.
  • The rest of the Overview. The three figures across the top, the new-users panel and what-is-on-today all stay exactly as they are. One panel is replaced and nothing else on that screen moves.
  • Redesigning the old sale page. It keeps its three kinds of sale, its tabs and its habits. We route around it and link to it; we do not open it up.
  • Failed retail and one-off invoice payments. Only membership payments appear in the panel and only contract invoices get the new page. A card declined at the till is a different problem with a different urgency.
  • Changing the automatic attempt schedule. How many attempts, on which days, and the messages that already go out with them are all untouched. We add a manual retry beside the schedule; we do not tune the schedule.
  • Chasing money outside the app. No text messages, no dialler, no dunning campaign builder, no handing anything to a collections service. One message to the owner, one message to the member, and the two buttons.
