Why this matters

The framing

The problem this project sets out to solve, and how we will know it is solved — told in the words of the people who live it.

The struggling moment

Customers keep writing in to ask us to add a calendar to Basecamp. We called one of them — not to ask what a calendar should look like, but to ask when she wanted one. What was she doing at the moment the thought occurred to her?

She works in an office with a big calendar drawn on a chalkboard wall. Her officemates chalk up their client meetings on it, in the handful of meeting rooms they have. One day she was working from home when a client called and asked for a meeting.

The request was “add a calendar.” The story is asking for something much smaller and much more specific.

Everything below follows from that one sentence. The project is narrow because the problem is narrow — she needed to see free spaces, and the rest of what a calendar does never entered her afternoon.

Today's way, and where it breaks

Right now the office runs on two things. The wall carries the real schedule: who has a room, and when. And the schedule in Basecamp carries a list of events, one after another, in date order — deadlines, milestones, the things everyone needs to know are coming.

The list works for what it was built for. It breaks the moment the question is not “what is happening” but “what isn’t.” A free Wednesday has no row. It is simply the space between two rows, and the space between two rows looks exactly the same whether it is one empty day or five.

So she copes the way anyone would: she drives in and looks at the wall. That afternoon it cost her an hour of traffic and the answer was no anyway — there was no free space that worked for her client. What she was trying to do was not “use a calendar.” It was to give a client a time, on the phone, that she knew nobody else had taken.

The outcome test

Someone who is nowhere near the office is asked for a time, and answers from the schedule alone: one look, no call to a colleague standing in front of the wall, no drive in. Anyone can check this without us — ask a person who has never seen the feature to find the next free Wednesday, and watch whether they read anything at all before they answer.

The counter-check matters just as much. If the office still keeps the chalk version as the real one, we have not solved this, however good the screen looks.

Why now

We launched Basecamp 3 without a calendar, and the requests started arriving almost immediately. Until this call they were unanswerable: “add a calendar” is an idea with no edges, and we would have been guessing at which part of it anybody actually wanted. Now we have a specific moment to design against, and the same afternoon is happening in a lot of offices.

This pays in money, and specifically in money we already have. Nobody buys Basecamp to schedule things — past versions carried a calendar and only about a tenth of customers used it — so this is not going to sell an account. It removes a standing reason for a team to keep its real schedule somewhere else, and a team whose real schedule lives on a wall is a team using half of what it pays for.

The big hire and the little hires

The big hire is the first time somebody in that office chalks nothing on the wall and adds the meeting to the schedule instead. That switch does not happen because entering an event got easier — it did not change at all. It happens when reading the schedule back finally beats walking over to the wall: two months of dots that open from a kitchen table are worth more than chalk that only works if you are standing in the room.

The little hires are every “how does Thursday look?” after that. Each one has to be answered in a single look, on a page they already had open, with no view to choose and nothing to wait for. The day any of those moments costs a click into a day and a squint, the wall gets its job back.

What we will build

The shape

The solution at the level of what people see and do. The drawings are rough sketches of how it works, not of how it will look.

In plain language

The schedule grows a grid above the list it already has. Two months side by side, one cell per day. A day with something on it carries a dot for each thing; a day with nothing on it carries nothing, and that blank cell is the answer she called about. The list of events stays exactly where it is, underneath. Clicking a day scrolls that day into view in the list. Arrows at the top move the two-month window back and forward. That is the whole feature.

Read-only is carrying a lot of weight in that description. Nothing on the grid can be dragged, stretched, colored or edited. The grid is a picture of how full each day is, and every question deeper than that is answered by the list underneath, which is the list we already have.

▸ Query What the grid asks for

One query per window, counting in the database rather than in the page. The window is two months — at most sixty-two cells — so the result is tiny and fixed. Nothing to page, nothing to virtualize, nothing to cache. 

day_counts(window_start, window_end)
  -> [{ day date, events integer }]

Which day a dot belongs to is decided once, in the schedule’s own time zone, and the grid and the list use the same answer. An event at eleven at night sits on the day it starts, for everybody, whichever time zone they are reading from.

▸ Schema Where the day comes from

Events carry instants; the grid needs days. The conversion happens in one place, against the schedule’s own zone, so two people in two countries looking at the same grid see the same dots. 

schedule(
  id         identifier,
  time_zone  text
)

event(
  id          identifier,
  schedule_id identifier,
  title       text,
  starts_at   timestamp with time zone,
  ends_at     timestamp with time zone
)

The flow is one screen and one hop. Nothing new to navigate, nothing new to learn, and the place people already go for the schedule is the place this appears.

Walkthrough

The same afternoon, with this in place. She is at home; the client is on the phone, asking for a time next week.

  1. She opens the project’s schedule — the same page she has always had, with the same list of events on it.

  2. Above the list there are now two months of dots. This week is peppered with them. Next week has two dots on the Tuesday, one on the Thursday, and one day in the middle with nothing at all. She has not read a word.

  3. She clicks that Wednesday. The list underneath scrolls to where the day sits — the last thing before it, the next thing after it, nothing in between. The day really is open, and not just missing from her memory.

  4. She offers the client Wednesday at ten while they are still on the call, and adds the meeting from the list right there. It becomes a dot on Wednesday for everyone else looking at the same grid.

  5. She does not get in the car. The hour of traffic, and the drive that ended in no free space anyway, do not happen.

Time bombs

The places where this could blow up mid-build. Each one is defused here, in this document, before the build starts.

A day with a lot on it turns the cell into a smear of dots.
How we defuse it Dots stop at five. Past that the cell just reads as busy, which is all the grid has to say — it answers free or not free, and the list underneath carries the truth. No counter, no “+3 more,” no second row of dots.
Multi-day events want to become bars across the grid.
How we defuse it A multi-day event puts an ordinary dot on every day it covers, and that is the end of it. No bars, no spanning, no wrapping around the edge of the screen — the grid never needs to know an event lasts longer than a day, because it only ever counts days.
▸ Contract How a dot gets counted

One rule, applied when the counts are read, so a multi-day event and five one-day events are indistinguishable to the grid. 

days_of(event, window)
  -> every day from max(event.start_day, window_start)
                to min(event.end_day, window_end)
Which two months, and how you get to another pair, opens the whole date-picker question.
How we defuse it It opens on this month and the next, and the arrows move the window one month at a time. No date picker, no jumping to a year, no range to select. Someone scheduling a meeting next week never travels further than the arrow beside them.
Clicking a day with nothing on it has no obvious answer.
How we defuse it It scrolls the list to the gap where that day falls, between the day before and the day after, and does nothing else. No dialog, no form popping open, no empty state to design — seeing what brackets an empty day is exactly what makes it trustworthy.
Phones and desktops usually want different calendar interactions.
How we defuse it The read-only grid disarms this one: there is nothing to drag, so a tap and a click do the same thing. On a narrow screen the second month sits under the first and everything else is unchanged.

No-gos

Things this project deliberately does not do, so nobody has to wonder.

  • Dragging events between days to move them.
  • Dragging the edge of an event to change how long it lasts.
  • Spanning a multi-day event across the grid, or wrapping one around the edge of the screen. The dots repeat instead.
  • Color coding, categories, or any way to tell one kind of event from another.
  • Weekly and daily views. A month is the only scale this has.
  • Editing anything from the grid. It is read-only, without exception.
  • Anything about rooms. The wall tracks which meeting room is taken; this does not model a room, its availability, or a clash between two bookings. It shows which days have something on them.
