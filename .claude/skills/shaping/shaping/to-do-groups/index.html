To-Do Groups

Let any to-do list be split into named groups, so the fake divider rows people type today become a real part of the list.

Not built yet Built

A worked example: an illustrative shaping document derived from published source material, not a real project. Source: Shape Up by Ryan Singer, chapters 4–6 (skill/references/01-shape-up/05–07).

Why this matters

The framing

The problem this project sets out to solve, and how we will know it is solved — told in the words of the people who live it.

The struggling moment

It is the start of a cycle and a team lead is laying out the work for the iOS team. One list, twenty-eight items, and three different kinds of work inside it: the big batch, the small batch, and everything else. She wants the list to say that. The list has no way to say it, so she says it in the only place it lets her type — she adds a to-do that reads ============ BIG BATCH ============, then another that reads =========== SMALL BATCH ===========, then ====== OTHER ======.

Now the list looks right and counts wrong. Those three rows are to-dos. They have checkboxes. They sit inside the 18/28 at the top of the list. They can be assigned to somebody. Anyone on the team can tick one off and quietly delete the structure.

The same week, in another project, the same problem arrives wearing a different costume: a list of nineteen findings, every one of them typed with the same three characters in front of it. “QA: On small screens current plan label alignment tweak.” “QA: Discount combos read confusingly.” “QA: Frozen banner missing on /plans.” The prefix is the group. It has to be typed on every item, forever, by everybody.

Neither list is unusual, and neither is the person keeping it. Both are screenshots in the pitch this document is drawn from, sitting under one line: “For 13 years we’ve done stuff like this…”

Today's way, and where it breaks

A to-do list is one column of items in an order you can drag. A new item lands at the bottom and you drag it to where it belongs. When you check something off it leaves the column and joins a single collection of completed items at the bottom of the list. That is the whole model, and for a list of six items it is exactly right.

It breaks the moment one list carries more than one kind of work. Nothing in the list can hold a heading, so people make one out of the parts they have. Some type a row of equals signs and treat it as a divider. Some prepend a label to every item in a batch. Both work, and both cost something. A divider made of equals signs is a to-do: it counts toward the total, it can be checked off by mistake, it can be assigned to a person who then wonders what they are supposed to do with it. A label typed onto every item is retyping, on every item, by every person, forever — and it is still one flat list to whoever reads it next.

The third coping move is to stop fighting and split the work across several lists. That scatters one piece of work over the page, and the counts at the top of each list stop adding up to anything anyone cares about.

The workarounds are also not something to be proud of. As the pitch puts it, “it’s not a refined pattern to share with customers.”

The outcome test

A month after this ships, open the busiest lists in the company. On any list that uses groups, nobody has typed a row of equals signs or a “QA:” prefix to make a section; the count at the top counts only real work; and anyone who asks “what is left in QA?” can get a page with just that group on it in one click. Anybody can check this without asking us: look for the equals signs.

Why now

This pays in time first. Every list that outgrows a screen re-invents the same hack, and every hack is maintained by hand from then on — retyped prefixes, dividers dragged back into place, counts nobody trusts. It pays in credibility second: the to-do list is the screen we put in front of people most often, and the workaround is now visible in the lists we share with customers. The pattern is thirteen years old and it has stopped being a private embarrassment. As the pitch says, “13 years in, it’s time to level up.”

The big hire and the little hires

The big hire is the first group somebody makes instead of typing a divider row. Two things have to be true at that moment. Making a group has to be one action away from where they already are, which is the list itself — not a settings screen, not a new kind of document. And the rest of the list must not move under them. Nobody trades a working hack for a feature that reorganizes their week.

The little hires are the rest of the cycle. Every time they add a to-do, it has to land in the section they were looking at, or they will go back to adding at the bottom and dragging. Every time they check something off, its group has to survive the check-off, or the history stops being readable. Every time they scan, the list has to answer “what is left in QA?” without them reading every line. And on lists where nobody wants any of this, the list has to be exactly what it was yesterday.

What we will build

The shape

The solution at the level of what people see and do. The drawings are rough sketches of how it works, not of how it will look.

In plain language

A list with no groups is untouched. Same rows, same order, same buttons, same behaviour — “to-dos without groups will look identical to today’s to-dos. We won’t add any additional UI around them, no new buttons, etc…” (chapter 6). Everything below is what happens only once somebody asks for structure.

Structure comes from one new thing: a divider. A divider is a named line you can put anywhere in a list. It is not a to-do — there is nothing to check, no one to assign it to, no date on it. What it does is split the list.

Nothing here needs a second kind of list, and nothing needs a group to be a thing you create somewhere else and then fill. A divider is a row in the same ordered column the to-dos already live in, and which group a to-do is in is a question about where it sits.

▸ Schema What a group is made of

A divider is not a new table. It is a row in the list’s existing ordered collection, marked as a divider instead of a to-do, so ordering, dragging, and permissions come along for free. 

list_row(
  id            uuid,
  list_id       uuid,
  kind          'todo' | 'divider',
  title         text,
  position      decimal,
  completed_at  timestamp null,   -- always null on a divider
  completed_in  uuid null         -- the divider it sat under when it was checked off
)

There is no group_id on a to-do. A group is a span, not a label: the group of a row is the nearest divider above it, and completed_in exists only so a to-do that has left the column can still say where it came from.

There are two ways to make a divider, and they match the two ways people already talk about it: “I want a section here” and “these five things belong together.” The first is an item in the list’s own menu, which drops a blank divider at the end of the loose to-dos and puts the cursor in its name. The second is selecting several loose to-dos and grouping them, which creates an unnamed divider and puts them underneath it.

Once a divider exists, the section under it gets its own way to add work. Otherwise every new to-do lands at the bottom of the list and has to be dragged home — the pitch calls that out as the whole point: “It’s much better to add it place when we have places to add” (chapter 6).

And a group’s name is a link. Clicking it opens a page with just that group’s to-dos on it, which is the answer to “what is left in QA?” without reading the whole list.

▸ Contract How a to-do knows which group it is in

One function, read at display time, and every behaviour in this document falls out of it. 

group_of(row) =
  the last row before `row` in position order
  whose kind is 'divider'
  — or none, if there is no divider above it

Because it is derived and not stored, moving a divider moves its group, deleting a divider merges its to-dos into whatever is above them, and dragging a to-do above the first divider makes it loose. None of those is a feature anyone has to write.

Walkthrough

The same lead, the same list, the same start of a cycle — with this in place.

  1. She opens the list. Twenty-five to-dos in one column, no equals signs, nothing on the screen that was not there last cycle.

  2. She selects the four big-batch items and chooses to group them. A divider appears above them with its name waiting; she types Big batch and the four items are now under it.

  3. For the second one she goes the other way: the list’s menu, then Create a group. A blank divider lands at the end of the loose to-dos and scrolls into view; she names it Small batch and drags three items under it.

  4. What is left sits above both dividers. Those are the loose to-dos, and they stay at the top because that is what being above the first divider means. She does not arrange anything.

  5. On Wednesday a QA finding comes in. She is already looking at the QA section, so she adds it there, and she does not type “QA:” in front of it — the section it is in already says that.

  6. She checks three things off. They leave their sections and join the one collection of completed items at the bottom, each now reading “Design: …” or “QA: …” — the prefix people used to type by hand, now written by the list, in the one place it was actually useful.

  7. On Friday someone asks what is left in QA. She clicks the group’s name and hands over a page with just that group on it. The count at the top of the list reads 18/25: only real work.

Time bombs

The places where this could blow up mid-build. Each one is defused here, in this document, before the build starts.

Completed to-dos have no obvious home once the list has sections.
How we defuse it Leave them exactly where they are. Completed items keep collecting in one place at the bottom of the whole list, ungrouped, and each one that was in a group gets the group’s name in front of it. Unchecking one puts it back in its group, as long as the divider still exists. This is the one deliberately messy corner of the concept, and it is messy on purpose: re-rendering completed items inside each group drags in navigation, paging, and performance questions that have swallowed this idea before.
Someone deletes a divider.
How we defuse it The to-dos under it join whatever is above them — the previous group, or the loose block if it was the first divider. Nothing is deleted with the divider and nothing needs confirming, because the divider was never holding the to-dos, only marking where a section starts.
Someone drags a divider up or down the list.
How we defuse it The group moves with it. A group is the run of to-dos between one divider and the next, so picking a divider up picks up everything under it, which is what anyone dragging a section by its heading expects.
Dragging across the line between loose and grouped needs rules nobody can remember.
How we defuse it There are no rules to remember, and nothing to forbid. Above the first divider is loose; below a divider belongs to it. Drag a to-do up past the first divider and it becomes loose; drag a divider to the very top and the list is all groups. Position decides everything, so there is no state to police and no drag to block.
New to-dos land at the bottom and have to be dragged into the right section.
How we defuse it Every section carries its own way to add a to-do, including the loose block at the top. Add where you are looking, and nothing needs moving afterwards.

The patch is lifted straight from the book’s own account of shaping this project: “We would leave the completed items exactly as they worked previously. Instead of grouping or segmenting them, we would just append the name of the group to each completed item” (chapter 5).

No-gos

Things this project deliberately does not do, so nobody has to wonder.

  • No groups inside groups and no indenting — “just as many one-level groups as you’d like.”
  • No colour coding. It would make the page more interesting and it is not what anybody is stuck on; the book cut it from this same project for the same reason.
  • No re-segmenting the completed collection by group. That is the time bomb above, and it stays defused.
  • Dividers are not to-dos: no checkbox, no assignee, no due date, no comments, no attachments.
  • No to-do templates that arrive with groups already in place. It is the obvious next thing and it is a different project.

How the build starts

The goal

The text that ignites the build, ready to paste, with a live count against its 4,000-character limit. 

To-Do Groups — Let any to-do list be split into named groups, so the fake divider rows people type today become a real part of the list.

Before anything else, read shaping/to-do-groups/index.html in full. It is the single source of truth for this project — the problem, the shape, every time bomb with its solution, and the boundaries. Nothing below repeats it.

Done means all of this is true:

- On a list that uses groups, nobody has to type a row of equals signs or a "QA:" prefix to make a section, and the count at the top of the list counts only real work.
- Anyone who asks what is left in one group can open a page with just that group's to-dos on it, in one click from the group's name.
- A list with no groups looks and behaves exactly as it does today: no new buttons, no new panel, no behaviour change.
- The document's walkthrough replays in the shipped product, step for step: select several loose to-dos and group them; create a group from the list's menu and name the blank divider where it lands; loose to-dos stay above the first group without anyone arranging them; each section adds its own to-dos; checking one off moves it to the single collection at the bottom with its group name in front of it; unchecking returns it to its group while the divider still exists; the group's name opens the group's own page.

Constraints:

- Branch off main.
- The no-gos in the document are binding: no groups inside groups, no indenting, no colour coding, no re-segmenting the completed collection, no dividers that behave like to-dos, no templates.
- Every time bomb in the document already carries its solution — build the solution that is written there. If one of them does not hold up once you are in the code, stop and say so rather than inventing a way around it.
- When it ships, flip the document's built bit: data-built="true" in shaping/to-do-groups/index.html.
