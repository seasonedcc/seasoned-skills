Why this matters

The framing

The problem this project sets out to solve, and how we will know it is solved — told in the words of the people who live it.

The struggling moment

Daniel stewards the workflow this repository ships: the written instructions — the skills — that our agents read before doing any piece of work. By late August 2026 the backlog of recorded lessons had reached 184 open issues, most arriving with suggested wording ready to fold in, and Daniel had a clear vision for improvements beyond them. He opened a shaping session to plan that next round — and stopped before the first question. Almost every lesson would land as a new paragraph on instructions that are already too long: the corpus stands near 74,000 words, the orchestration skill alone near 9,500, and an agent that reads too much before starting works measurably worse. Folding the lessons in as filed would make the workflow worse, not better.

Today's way, and where it breaks

New instructions arrive from two directions. When a skill is born, someone on the team asks an agent to write it — and the agent writes with no guide for how agent-facing prose should sound or how long it may run: the workflow has exactly such a guide for its human-facing pages, and nothing for the far larger body of prose the agents themselves read. And every finished task runs a self-improvement pass that files its lessons as additions: a new rule, a new warning, a new paragraph, each individually reasonable. Nothing pushes the other way. No tool measures how big a skill has grown; the one size rule on the books — keep a skill under about five thousand words — sits inside a skill and is enforced by nothing, and the largest skill is nearly double it.

It breaks in review. These instructions are for humans as much as for agents: a person reviews every change to them, and a person is who each rule must convince. Yet the prose arrives in an AI dialect — a register dense with the agents’ own shorthand — that the reviewer has to translate before they can even judge, and the workflow’s existing guide for human-facing pages, whose translation table makes exactly this dialect mechanical to spot, stops at the docs and never reaches the skills. Size raises the wall higher still: judging a change means holding the whole skill in your head to see whether the new rule is already there in other words, contradicts a neighbor, or belongs somewhere else — and at today’s sizes nobody can, so additions pass and deletions almost never happen. The record shows where that leads: one lesson codified across ten separate issues in as many places, skills that contradict themselves, and rules kept alive only to compensate for weaknesses long gone.

The projects that use the workflow repeat the pattern on their own side: each adds its local instructions on top, with no bound on how much, and upgrades never prune what the package has since absorbed — so the same rule ends up stated twice, in slightly different words, drifting apart.

The growth has a direction bias, too. Nearly every filed lesson is born from something going wrong, so the corpus teaches prevention far more than it teaches creation — and models and people alike drift into guarding the downside above all. Preventing mistakes matters, but it is not what the instructions are for: their highest return is teaching how to produce amazing work and how to invent, and any rewrite of them must be judged by the upside it creates before the failures it prevents.

One truth bounds everything above: the workflow, big skills and all, works. It carries our projects every day, and the failures its rules exist to prevent stay prevented. The problem is what the size costs and what it blocks — never what the instructions do for us. Any cure that loses what they do is worse than the disease.

The outcome test

Four things have to be true afterwards, each checkable without asking us.

  1. Nothing that works today is lost. The workflow keeps doing for us what it does now — it is doing wonders even with the big skills, and no slimming is worth breaking that. The slimmed instructions still deliver everything the old ones deliver: the quality of work they teach, the inventions the improvement channel keeps producing, and the failures they prevent. The observable trace: in the efforts that follow, the work stays at the bar we hold today, and the lessons the corpus already carries do not get re-learned the hard way and re-filed as new issues — a re-filed lesson we once had is the alarm that a cut went too deep.

  2. Improvement passes invent. The pass that closes each effort does more than correct what went wrong: it proposes upside of its own — new skills, new abstractions in the product itself, refactors that would change what the system can become, whole new systems. Because most inventions deserve shaping before they are built, the pass files the ones it judges shaping-worthy as issues on the project’s own repository — proposals waiting for a shaping session — not only as ready-made changes. An orchestrator can design solutions humans would not think of, but only if it knows inventing is part of its job. The observable trace: the projects’ queues begin carrying agent-proposed inventions that surprise the people who read them.

  3. The postponed session opens. The day this ships, Daniel starts the shaping session this project displaced — working the backlog of recorded improvements — and folding a lesson into a skill no longer fights the corpus. The exact session that could not open in August opens.

  4. Budgets hold mechanically. Every skill, and every consuming project’s local additions, fits a size budget enforced by tooling: a change that would breach the budget fails a check — in this repository and in every consumer — instead of depending on anyone’s discipline or memory.

  5. Changes become reviewable, and deletion becomes normal. A person can read any skill, and judge any proposed instruction change, in one sitting — the prose in plain language, with nothing to translate. And over the months after shipping, the passes that codify lessons delete and rewrite as often as they add.

Why now

Every improvement we want to make — the 184 recorded lessons and the vision beyond them — lands as prose on this same corpus, so until the corpus can absorb change without growing sicker, every other project stays queued behind this one. The cost also compounds on its own: every agent, on every task, in every project that uses the workflow, reads these instructions before starting work, and the pile grows with each finished effort — the newest third of the backlog arrived from a single one.

The big hire and the little hires

The big hire is Daniel’s, made once: adopting the new discipline into the workflow — the writing guide as a binding rule, the size budgets as enforced checks, the reorganized skills as the new baseline. What earns that switch is the outcome test’s first item: proof that nothing the workflow does for us today was lost in the slimming.

The little hires are made daily, and they are where this lives or dies. Every agent writing instructions — a skill rewrite, an improvement pass, a project’s local additions — has to choose the guide’s plain, sized prose over the dialect it would produce by default; the guide and the checks must make the right way the easy way. And every person reviewing an instruction change has to keep finding that one sitting suffices — the day a review requires translation again, rubber-stamping returns, and with it the additive-only drift. Agents reliably re-hire whatever the enforced path is, so the little hires that can genuinely be lost are the human ones.

What we will build

The shape

The solution at the level of what people see and do. The drawings are rough sketches of how it works, not of how it will look.

In plain language

One rule is settled and anchors everything else: every change to the instructions argues its case, in the change itself, whichever direction it points. An addition names the upside it creates or the live failure it prevents, and shows that the rule is not already on the books in other words and would not serve better as tooling. A deletion argues why the text is dead: it compensated for a weakness that is gone, duplicates a rule stated elsewhere, has been absorbed into tooling, or teaches nothing about creating the upside or preventing a live failure. The same bar binds the slimming passes of this project and every improvement pass that follows it — detection stays as the backstop, but every cut and every growth defends itself at the moment it is made.

A second settled element: the improvement pass deduplicates before it files. It searches the existing issues first, and when the lesson is already recorded it adds the new case as a comment on that issue instead of opening a near-duplicate — so demand weight accumulates in one place instead of hiding across copies.

A third settled element resolves a conflict with the workflow’s standing rules in the open. The workflow’s rules ban filing issues as idea storage, after the method’s own no-backlogs principle — and this project teaches improvement passes to file inventions as issues. The two coexist because issue-tracking serves exactly two purposes here: surfacing urgent problems — the cord anyone pulls to stop the line — and accumulating raw material for framing and shaping sessions. What the ban forbids is the third use: a board of tasks developers are expected to work through. Issues are signals and demand records, never assignments; nobody owes an issue a build, and a shaping session reads them the way this project read its 184. An agent’s inventions belong there for one more reason: unlike a person’s, an agent’s important idea cannot come back on its own — its memory ends with the session — so the issue is the only door it can return through. The build rewrites the standing rule about idea storage to say exactly this.
