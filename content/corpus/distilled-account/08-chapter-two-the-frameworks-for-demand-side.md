# The frameworks for demand-side selling — a distilled account

This is a stand-in for the book's chapter two, written in our own words. It
teaches the frameworks the shaping skill cites from that chapter, at the depth
the citations lean on. The short verbatim quotes the skill carries — the
four-forces sentence and the built-to-be-bought-and-used sentence — are the
book's own words and appear only in the skill; everything below is paraphrase.

## Supply side versus demand side

The chapter's founding distinction is between two ways of looking at the same
sale. The supply side starts from the product: its features, its price, its
positioning, and how to push all of that toward a buyer. The demand side starts
from the person: the progress they are trying to make in their particular
circumstances, and what has to be true before they will change how they do
things today. On the demand side, nobody buys because a product exists or
because it is impressive — they buy because they are struggling with the way
things currently work and the product looks like progress. The practical
consequence is a change of study object: instead of studying the product, you
study the struggle, in the customer's own context and language.

## The struggling moment

Demand begins at a struggling moment: a concrete point in time when the current
way of doing something stops being good enough for a specific person. Without
that moment there is no energy for change, and no amount of feature work
creates it. With it, the person starts looking — often clumsily, often at
first for a better version of what they already do. The struggling moment is
why the shaping process insists on one specific story rather than a persona or
a list of complaints: the moment is where the demand actually lives, and
everything else about a project is accountable to it.

## Jobs to be done

The chapter frames purchases through jobs to be done: people "hire" a product
to make a specific kind of progress in a specific circumstance, and they "fire"
it when it stops serving that progress. The job is defined by the person's
situation and desired outcome, not by the product's category — which is why
solutions to the same job can look wildly different, and why the set of real
competitors is discovered by asking what people actually do today, not by
scanning the product's market. Jobs are stable over long periods; the things
hired to do them come and go.

## The four forces

Whether a person actually switches is decided by four forces in tension. Two
promote the change: the **push** of the current situation — how painful the
struggling moment is — and the **pull** (the book calls it magnetism) of the
new solution — how attractive the imagined better life looks. Two resist it:
the **anxiety** of the new — will it work, what will it cost me, what if I
choose wrong — and the **habit** of the present — the comfort, sunk learning,
and inertia of the current way, however flawed. The chapter's rule, which the
shaping skill quotes verbatim, is that the promoting pair has to outweigh the
resisting pair before anyone buys. The force diagram is drawn as two arrows
pushing toward the change and two pushing back; shaping reaches for it when
adoption risk is real, because it turns a vague worry about "will they use it"
into four nameable, addressable quantities.

## The big hire and the little hires

The chapter distinguishes the moment a product is bought from the moments it
is used. The **big hire** is the purchase or adoption decision — made once,
often by someone weighing the four forces at their strongest. The **little
hires** happen every time the person chooses to use the product again, day
after day; each one is a small re-hiring, and a product that keeps losing them
gets quietly fired no matter how the big hire went. The design consequence —
the sentence the skill quotes verbatim — is that a product has to be built to
win both: to be bought, and to be used. In shaping, this becomes a framing
question with two answers by name: who approves this once, and who has to
choose it again every working day.
